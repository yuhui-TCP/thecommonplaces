ISSUE Nº004 — THE ACHIEVEMENT TRAP

When success is always the next milestone, when do you get to have arrived?

The Achievement Trap — why crossing the finish line rarely feels like the finish line, and what a life built entirely on the next milestone quietly forgets to ask

For people who have never once said “I’ve made it” and meant it. We begin with a question — about ambition, satisfaction, and what finishing actually requires — then follow the thread through its backstory, its complications, the ideas it opens up, and how they take shape in the real world.

Structure: 5-Step Journey Reading Time: ~18 min Subject: Ambition & Satisfaction
01 Question

1. The Question

The night of your fortieth birthday, close to midnight.

The dinner is over, the candles blown out somewhere around nine, and by now you're home, phone still lit up with messages you haven't replied to. Forty was supposed to mean something specific — not a party trick, but an actual checkpoint, a number by which certain things were meant to be settled. Somewhere in your twenties you built a rough mental list of what forty should look like: the career at a certain altitude, the savings at a certain size, a sense of having roughly figured out who you are. Tonight, running through the list almost involuntarily, most of the boxes are, technically, ticked.

And yet the feeling that was supposed to arrive along with the ticked boxes hasn't. There's no swelling sense of arrival, no quiet inner voice confirming that you've made it — just the oddly familiar sensation of already, faintly, wondering what the next ten years are supposed to add. By the time you've plugged your phone in for the night, forty has already filed itself as a checkpoint rather than a destination — not because anyone told you to see it that way, but because you cannot remember the last time you saw a milestone as anything else.

This is a familiar sensation in a country that has built an unusually complete architecture around the idea that effort should be rewarded with advancement. The PSLE score that determines the school. The school that shapes the CCA, the network, the university options. The scholarship bond, the first job, the first promotion, the corner office, the next title. Singapore does not merely tolerate ambition — it has spent sixty years building the infrastructure to reward it efficiently, transparently, and relentlessly. By most measures, this has worked extraordinarily well.

And yet ask a thoughtful forty-year-old in this city how the number actually felt once they arrived at it, and “satisfied” is rarely the word that surfaces. You hear something closer to a brief, genuine lift — followed almost immediately by a quiet recalibration of what the new baseline now is, and what still needs to be chased from here.

That recalibration is worth sitting with, because it is easy to misdiagnose. The instinctive explanation is that you are simply ambitious, perhaps too ambitious, and that the fix is to want less. The more accurate explanation is narrower and less flattering: you were never taught to distinguish between a goal that was supposed to change how you feel, and a goal that was only ever supposed to change your position relative to everyone else.

This distinction is not a technicality. It changes what the actual problem is.

For most of human history, achievement was scarce and largely involuntary — survival, status, and rank were assigned by birth, caste, or circumstance far more often than they were earned. Today, in a meritocracy engineered as deliberately as Singapore's, advancement is abundant, visible, and genuinely available to almost anyone willing to chase it. What hasn't gotten any easier — what may, if anything, have gotten harder to notice — is knowing which of the things you're chasing were ever going to make you feel like you'd arrived, and which were only ever going to move the finish line further out.

Call it the arrival deficit, to separate it from ordinary ambition. Ordinary ambition wants something specific and stops, satisfied, once it has it. The arrival deficit wants the feeling that having something was supposed to produce — and keeps reaching for the next achievement because the last one, mysteriously, didn't deliver it.

Singapore's own success story makes this harder to question, not easier. This is a nation that quite literally narrates its history as a triumph of relentless striving — from a resource-poor port to a global financial centre inside a single lifetime, achieved by people who kept moving the goalposts on themselves, on purpose, because standing still was never presented as an option. To ask whether all that goalpost-moving might be costing something can feel, in this context, faintly ungrateful — like criticising the very instinct that built the comfortable life you're now living inside.

But it's worth asking a question the meritocracy story doesn't often invite: whether the next milestone was ever actually going to be the one that felt like enough. Perhaps the goal was never to stop achieving — for many people, achievement is genuinely, honestly, part of what a good life is made of. Perhaps the actual skill, the one nobody teaches directly, is learning to tell which milestones were chosen because they would mean something to you, and which were only ever chosen because they would look like something to everyone else.

This is the question this issue exists to sit with, and the question The Common Places will keep returning to, issue after issue: when success is always defined as the next milestone, how do you tell an achievement that was actually yours from one that was simply next in line?

Bridge Before assuming this restlessness is a uniquely Singaporean, uniquely modern condition, it's worth asking whether every society organised around advancement has quietly produced the same ache — long before PSLE existed to blame.
02 Backstory

2. The Backstory

The Wolf of Wall Street

Martin Scorsese's 2013 film, based on Jordan Belfort's own memoir, offers a useful corrective to the idea that this anxiety is uniquely Singaporean.

Leonardo DiCaprio plays Belfort as a man for whom no number ever holds still long enough to feel like enough. A fortune that would let most people stop working for several lifetimes barely registers before the film has moved him on to the next deal, the next yacht, the next round of excess — not out of simple greed, but because stopping to feel satisfied was never actually part of the plan. Money, in Belfort's telling, was never the point. It was only ever the scoreboard.

The film's most telling detail isn't the mansions or the yacht — it's how quickly Belfort narrates his own, genuinely staggering income as though it were unremarkable, a number that would represent a lifetime's security to almost anyone else, delivered with the flat boredom of a man already thinking about the next figure. The arrival deficit, rendered exactly: a number that was supposed to mean “I've made it” instead simply resets what “enough” is allowed to mean.

The chase eventually costs Belfort his business, his marriage, and his freedom — not because he stopped pursuing more, but because the pursuit itself never had a natural stopping point built in. Tellingly, the real Belfort didn't stop chasing after prison, either; he simply found a new scoreboard, rebuilding himself as a motivational speaker teaching others how to sell. The instinct outlasted the fortune that was supposed to satisfy it.

This isn't only a Hollywood invention. Singapore, now one of the world's fastest-growing wealth management hubs, has its own quiet version of Belfort's scoreboard — portfolio size, banking tier, the size of the year-end bonus: figures rarely spoken about directly, but constantly, silently compared, and treated as the clearest available evidence of a life going well. The film's warning lands with particular weight in a city where so much of a life's progress is genuinely, literally measured in numbers on a statement.

What The Wolf of Wall Street captures is the same mechanism the arrival deficit runs on everywhere else: the number was never really the point. It was only ever a stand-in for a feeling — of having made it, of no longer needing to prove anything — that a number was never actually built to produce.

This restlessness runs deeper than one film. Singapore's meritocracy was not an accident that produced this anxiety as an unfortunate side effect — it was a deliberate, founding piece of policy design. A young nation with no natural resources decided, explicitly, that talent and effort would be the currency that determined advancement, regardless of birth, because it was the only currency the country actually had. That decision built extraordinary things. It also meant that failing to advance could never be quietly blamed on circumstance — in a genuine meritocracy, standing still reads, uncomfortably, as a verdict on you.

The clearest sign that this pressure is structural rather than personal is that the institutions themselves have tried, more than once, to defuse it. In 2021, the Ministry of Education replaced the PSLE's T-score — a system that ranked children against each other down to fine decimal points — with broader Achievement Level bands, explicitly to reduce the chase for the last mark. By 2026, the conversation has moved further still, with officials publicly naming the education system's competitive intensity as an “arms race” worth addressing at a structural level, not merely a parenting problem to be managed at home.

The comforting implication is that the arrival deficit is not a personal flaw in your character; it is closer to the predictable output of a system built, with real success, to keep everyone moving. The more challenging implication runs the other way: no scoring reform, however well designed, can fully resolve this, because the chase was never really about T-scores, promotions, or the number on a statement. Those were simply whatever the system had on hand to measure with.

Bridge If the chase for the next milestone is this deeply built into the system, it is only fair to ask whether the chase itself might be the wrong target for criticism — whether it was, in fact, the very thing that built everything worth having in the first place.
03 Complication

3. The Complication

The Case for the Fire in the Belly

Singapore's founding narrative is not, on its own terms, a cautionary tale. It is a story about a first generation that refused to be satisfied — that treated “good enough” as a genuine danger, and kept striving past the point where a more contented society might reasonably have stopped.

This is the argument that complicates everything the previous chapter implied. It surfaces regularly and sincerely in Singapore's public life — in National Day Rally speeches worrying aloud about complacency, in op-eds asking whether a generation raised in comfort still has the appetite that built the comfort in the first place, in the recurring, half-affectionate, half-anxious question of whether Singapore has become, in the language of its own leaders, too comfortable to stay hungry. The concern is rarely about achievement itself. It is about what happens to a small country with no natural advantages the moment it stops chasing the next one.

Put plainly: if the instinct to always want the next milestone is what took a nation from a swamp port to a global hub in a single lifetime, on what grounds does an individual get to opt out of the same instinct and call it wisdom rather than the earliest sign of the complacency everyone was warned about?

This is a genuinely uncomfortable question for a publication built around interrogating the chase, and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than argued around. The honest answer is not that ambition is the problem. Ambition, chosen and examined, has built most of what is genuinely good about this country and plenty of what is good in an individual life. The problem is narrower and easier to miss: a system can reward relentless striving at the national level while quietly failing to teach the individuals inside it how to tell a milestone that will actually satisfy them from one that will simply reset the clock.

Those are not the same skill, and Singapore has historically been extraordinary at teaching the first while never quite getting around to the second. The nation needed people who wouldn't stop striving. It never promised that striving, on its own, would feel like arriving — and it was never really the striving's job to.

04 Idea

4. The Idea

The Arrival Fallacy — Tal Ben-Shahar

Tal Ben-Shahar, who taught Harvard's most popular course on positive psychology, gave this exact mistake a name: the arrival fallacy — the mistaken belief that reaching a particular goal will produce a lasting change in how you feel.

Ben-Shahar's point is not that goals are worthless. It is that the emotional payoff we expect from crossing a finish line is almost always short-lived, regardless of how large the finish line is — and that most people respond to the letdown not by questioning the goal, but by immediately setting a bigger one, on the theory that this next one will finally be big enough to work.

Psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's self-determination theory offers the mechanism underneath Ben-Shahar's observation. Their research distinguishes intrinsic goals — mastery, connection, personal growth, contribution — from extrinsic goals — wealth, status, image, recognition. Attaining an intrinsic goal reliably improves wellbeing. Attaining an extrinsic goal, their studies repeatedly found, does not — and can, in some cases, be followed by a measurable dip, precisely at the moment the goal is achieved.

Few public examples make this as vivid as Joseph Schooling's own account of the months after Rio 2016. He had just delivered the single most extrinsic, most singular, most nationally celebrated goal available to a Singaporean athlete — the country's first Olympic gold, against his own childhood idol. In interviews afterward, he described the strange emptiness that followed, admitting in his own words that he “kind of lost myself” in the months after the medal, a feeling that followed him into a difficult stretch of competitive form. Years later, announcing his retirement, he pointed to complacency — not a lack of talent — as the mistake that had shaped the years after his greatest achievement.

This is the arrival fallacy playing out in public, at the highest possible stakes. If an Olympic gold medal — arguably the most extrinsic, most singularly validated achievement a human being can chase — was not enough to resolve the internal question on its own, it is worth asking honestly what a promotion, a bigger bonus, or a better title was ever realistically going to do.

A useful working rule falls out of combining Ben-Shahar with Deci and Ryan: not every achievement is chasing the same thing. Some milestones are intrinsic-adjacent — they reflect genuine mastery, contribution, or growth, and tend to feel like something even after the applause fades. Others are purely positional — valuable mainly, or only, in relation to what everyone else has — and tend to feel like nothing almost immediately, no matter how large they are. The actual skill, the one this whole issue is circling, is learning to sort your own ambitions into those two categories honestly, in advance, rather than discovering which kind a goal was only after you've already reached it.

Bridge Frameworks are easy to nod along to in the abstract. The harder question is what this looks like for someone whose entire career, family expectations, and self-image were built around chasing the next milestone without ever pausing to sort one kind from the other.
05 Practice

5. In Practice

Sorting the Milestones That Are Actually Yours

The problem with achievement today is not that there's too much of it available. It's that almost none of it arrives pre-labelled as intrinsic or positional. A promotion can be both a genuine step toward work you find meaningful and a number to report at the next family gathering. A scholarship can be both an honest opportunity and a line that settles, for a while, an unspoken question about your worth relative to your cohort. Most milestones worth having contain a little of both — which is exactly what makes them so easy to chase for the wrong half without ever noticing.

Consider how easily this happens without a single bad decision along the way. A first job is chosen partly for the work and partly for the name on the offer letter that will make the choice easy to explain at dinner. A further degree is pursued partly out of curiosity and partly because everyone in the peer group is quietly doing the same calculation. A seat on the leadership team is accepted partly for the mandate and partly because turning it down, once a peer already has one, would raise a question nobody wants to sit with. Each choice is individually reasonable. The pattern they add up to — a life measured almost entirely in positional terms — is not.

None of this is an argument for indifference, or for pretending status and comparison don't matter in a society that has, quite deliberately, built so much of its coordination around them. Wanting recognition, wanting to provide well, wanting your effort to be visible — none of that is a character flaw, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of dishonesty. The problem starts only when the positional half of a goal becomes the only reason left to chase it — when you can no longer say what the promotion, the degree, or the corner office would still be worth to you if no one else ever found out you'd gotten it.

The Common Places has no interest in telling you which milestones to want, or handing you a script for wanting less. What we're interested in is a simple, quieter test, applied honestly before the next milestone is chased rather than after: would this still feel worth having if no one else ever knew? For some goals, the honest answer is yes — the mastery, the craft, the relationship, the thing itself would still matter, applause or none. Those are worth everything you're prepared to give them. For others, the honest answer is a quieter no, and that isn't a reason for shame. It's simply information about which category a particular ambition belongs to, and a reason to stop expecting it to deliver a feeling it was never actually built to produce.

The goal was never to want less, or to feel guilty for wanting what you want. It is to stop letting the next milestone quietly decide, by default, what having arrived is even supposed to feel like — before an entire career has passed on the promise that the feeling was always one achievement away.

What We Chose Not to Include

We deliberately left out goal-setting frameworks, OKRs, and “how to find your passion” guides. Those tools can genuinely help you chase a goal more effectively. But technique was never the question this issue was built to answer. Before you set any goal more efficiently, the harder and prior question stands unanswered: which of your ambitions were ever going to feel like something, and which were only ever going to reset the clock?

We also chose not to publish a straightforward list of “milestones worth chasing in your thirties.” That format hands over a set of conclusions instead of a way of reasoning — the same trick the arrival deficit already plays on you, dressed up as advice. The purpose of this issue was never to tell you what to want. It was to make the difference between an intrinsic goal and a positional one visible enough that you can start telling them apart yourself.

The Common Places is not trying to make you want less. It is trying to help you notice, before the next milestone arrives, whether it was ever going to be the one that finally felt like enough.
