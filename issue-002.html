ISSUE Nº002 — THE CONVENIENCE TRAP

When ease is designed into everything, what happens to the parts of life that only effort could give you?

The Convenience Trap — why the easiest choice isn't always the one worth making, and what a frictionless life quietly takes from us

For people whose lives have quietly been engineered to require less and less of them — one tap, one swipe, one delivery away from almost anything. We begin with a question — about ease, effort, and what convenience quietly costs — then follow the thread through its backstory, its complications, the ideas it opens up, and how they take shape in the real world.

Structure: 5-Step Journey Reading Time: ~18 min Subject: Friction & Effort
01 Question

1. The Question

Saturday morning, 9:00am.

Nothing on the calendar.

Breakfast arrives before you've finished showering — ordered from bed, half asleep, in under a minute. The laundry bag by the door is collected, washed, folded and returned before dinner, without a single conversation. Groceries for the week assemble themselves into a cart from a saved list and arrive in a two-hour window you barely remember choosing. A parcel shows up at the door from an order you placed on impulse, then forgot.

By 11am, you have not touched a stove, joined a queue, haggled with a stranger, or made a single decision more effortful than picking a delivery slot. Every task that might once have required you — your hands, your patience, your presence — has been quietly reassigned to someone, or something, else.

This is a fitting situation for a country that built its entire modern identity on the promise that things should simply work. Singapore's efficiency is not incidental; it is close to the national story — the MRT that almost runs on schedule, the government service completed on a phone screen in minutes, the QR code that has made cash feel almost like an inconvenience of its own. By most global measures, we live inside one of the most frictionless societies that has ever existed.

And yet ask that same capable professional how their Saturday actually felt, and “satisfying” is rarely the word that surfaces. More often it's something closer to a low, formless flatness — the day passed, nothing went wrong, and somehow nothing much happened either.

That flatness is worth sitting with, because it is easy to misdiagnose. The instinctive explanation is that life has simply gotten too easy, and that the fix is to make it harder again, indiscriminately, as a kind of penance. The more accurate explanation is narrower and more useful: some of what got made effortless was pure waste, correctly removed — and some of it was never a problem to begin with. We have mostly stopped being able to tell the difference.

This distinction is not a technicality. It changes what the actual problem is.

For most of human history, the scarce resource was effort itself — getting food, getting clean clothes, getting anywhere, took real, unavoidable labour. Today, that labour is optional in ways earlier generations could not have imagined. What hasn't gotten any cheaper — what may, if anything, have gotten harder to notice — is knowing which of that labour we were actually meant to keep.

Call it the convenience paradox, to separate it from simple laziness or busyness. Every app, subscription and service is competing to make the same claim: this will make your life easier. We rarely stop to ask, easier for what — and at what quiet cost to the rest of it.

Singapore's own self-image makes this a harder question to sit with, rather than easier. This is a nation whose founding story is, in large part, about engineering friction out of daily life — building systems that simply function, when so much of the region around it may not. To question convenience here can feel faintly ungrateful, like criticising the very machinery that built the comfortable life you are now living inside.

But it is worth asking a question that the efficiency story doesn't often invite: whether frictionless is the same thing as good. Perhaps a life engineered to require nothing of you was never actually the goal. Perhaps what is slowly being traded away, one convenient tap at a time, is the very friction that used to tell us we were paying attention to our own lives.

This is the question this issue exists to sit with, and the question The Common Places will keep returning to, issue after issue: when everything is engineered to require nothing from you, what happens to the parts of life that only effort could give you?

Bridge Before treating this as a uniquely modern, uniquely Singaporean anxiety, it is worth asking whether every efficient society has faced the same trade — long before an app existed to make it instant.
02 Backstory

2. The Backstory

The Tyranny of Convenience — Tim Wu

Tim Wu's 2018 essay of the same name offers a useful corrective to the idea that this tension began with the smartphone.

Long before delivery apps, Wu traces the origins of modern convenience to the industrial efficiency movement of the late 19th century — the same logic that streamlined the factory floor was soon marketed for the household: ready-made meals, mail-order catalogues, and appliances that promised to return hours of drudgery to their owners. Once convenience became something you could sell, it stopped being a private preference and became a force shaping entire industries, then entire cultures.

Wu's sharper claim is not that convenience is bad, but that it is never neutral. Convenience compounds: the easier one option becomes, the more the alternatives quietly disappear — not because anyone weighed them and found them wanting, but because they simply stopped feeling thinkable. Ordering food becomes so frictionless that cooking starts to feel like an event rather than a habit. Paying with a tap becomes so automatic that handling cash starts to feel like an inconvenience rather than the default it always was. The most convenient option doesn't just win a single choice; it reshapes which choices remain available at all.

Wu is careful to say this isn't an argument for hardship as a virtue — refusing convenience on principle, for its own sake, would be its own kind of foolishness. His concern is narrower and more unsettling: that a life optimised entirely around ease tends, without anyone intending it, toward sameness. When the frictionless path is always taken, the distinctive, effortful, occasionally inconvenient choices that used to express who a person actually was start to fall away, one convenient decision at a time.

This history lands with particular weight in a city built on the promise that things should simply work. Singapore's Smart Nation ambitions, its near-universal digital payments, its government services collapsed into a single app — these are, by most measures, genuine achievements, the product of decades of deliberate design. Wu's account is a quiet corrective to the assumption that removing friction is an unqualified good every time it is technically possible. Every efficient society eventually discovers the same thing: the flood of convenience cannot be stopped, and arguably shouldn't be. The real question was never whether to have it. It was always what to do with everything it quietly displaces on its way in.

The comforting implication is that the flatness described in the last chapter is not a personal failing — it is closer to a structural feature of frictionless living itself. The more challenging implication runs the other way: no new app, subscription or service is likely to resolve this tension, because each one simply moves the trade-off further out of view rather than removing it.

Bridge If convenience has quietly been reshaping choices since the age of the factory, it is only fair to hear from those who insist convenience was never a private indulgence to feel guilty about. For a great many people, it was never the enemy of a meaningful life — it was the condition of having any life to speak of at all.
03 Complication

3. The Complication

Whose Convenience, Exactly — Angela Glover Blackwell

The curb-cut effect, first named by the policy scholar Angela Glover Blackwell, complicates the previous chapter more than it first appears to.

In the early 1970s, a small group of disabled students in Berkeley, tired of waiting for the city to act, poured their own crude ramps into the street corners near campus so wheelchairs could cross without being lifted over a kerb. It was activism before it was infrastructure. Decades later, those ramps — now a legal requirement on nearly every street corner — quietly serve almost everyone: parents pushing strollers, delivery riders with trolleys, travellers dragging luggage, anyone on a bicycle or a scooter. Blackwell's observation is that this pattern repeats constantly: features designed to remove a barrier for one marginalised group end up benefiting nearly everybody, who rarely stop to notice where the convenience came from.

Convenience, in other words, was rarely invented purely for the comfortable. It has just as often been an access ramp built for someone who needed it far more urgently than the person now enjoying it without a second thought.

This complicates the previous chapter considerably. To lament convenience in the general, philosophical sense risks flattening a long list of very different situations into a single undifferentiated complaint — and the flattening cuts both ways. For an elderly resident managing several medications on a fixed budget, an app that consolidates errands into one trip could be the difference between staying independent and not — assuming she can navigate it in the first place; for many elderly users, the unfamiliarity of the interface is itself a barrier, so the convenience these apps promise isn't equally available to everyone. For a foreign domestic worker whose one rest day a week is the only time that belongs to her, a food delivery order might seem like a reclaimed hour, but in practice this option is often out of reach — cost, restricted access to a phone or bank account, or an employer's rules make it far less likely she'll ever place that order. For a parent working two jobs, a service that removes one chore from an impossible day may be the reason the household keeps standing at all. The point, then, is not that convenience is uniformly redemptive, but that its value — and its accessibility — varies enormously depending on who is using it and what stands between them and the app itself.

There is a class dimension here worth naming plainly. The freedom to reject convenience — to cook everything from scratch, to queue in person on principle, to do things the harder way as a matter of taste — is itself often a form of privilege: the privilege of having enough time, health and money that friction becomes a choice rather than a cost imposed on you. Romanticising inconvenience from a comfortable seat is a genuinely different position from being denied convenience while everyone else enjoys it freely.

So the real complication is not that Wu was wrong. It is that the case against convenience only holds together at a certain altitude — as a general, philosophical observation about a society. Brought down to specific lives, “convenience is eroding meaning” and “convenience is what makes participation possible” turn out to be two entirely different claims, wearing the same word.

Bridge This doesn't dissolve the original worry — it sharpens it. If convenience is not one thing but several, the real design question isn't whether to have it, but which frictions are worth keeping on purpose, and which were simply waste we had mistaken for progress.
04 Idea

4. The Idea

Not All Friction Is the Same — Cass R. Sunstein

Sunstein's argument, distilled to its essential move, is that the word “friction” has been asked to do too much work. Some friction — the forms, the queues, the confusing procedures that serve no one's interest but a bureaucracy's own inertia — he calls sludge: a pure tax on people's time and dignity, worth eliminating wherever possible, and especially harmful to people with the least time to spare. But other friction is doing real work. A cooling-off period before a large purchase, a deliberate pause before an irreversible decision, an in-person step for something that genuinely deserves scrutiny — these frictions exist on purpose, to protect judgment from impulse. Removing them isn't progress; it's just a different kind of harm, arriving later.

This reframes the entire convenience debate. It was never a straightforward contest between friction and no friction. It is a design decision — made mostly by default, mostly without anyone choosing it consciously — about which parts of a day are allowed to run on autopilot, and which parts a person insists on doing the effortful way, on purpose, because the effort is where something they actually value happens to live.

For a Singapore professional, this carries an implication that is easy to skip past. Every convenience accepted without examination is simultaneously a small, mostly invisible decision about what is no longer being practised, no longer being noticed, no longer requiring your presence. None of that is inherently wrong — plenty of tasks were always just logistics, and removing the friction from them is a straightforward gain. The question worth asking, task by task, is which category a given piece of friction actually belongs to.

A useful companion to Sunstein's framework comes from behavioural research on what has been called the IKEA effect: people consistently value things more — sometimes considerably more — when they have assembled or built them themselves, compared to an identical, pre-assembled version of the same object. Effort, in other words, is not only a cost to be minimised. It is one of the mechanisms by which an outcome becomes genuinely yours. Strip the effort out entirely, and you may also be quietly stripping out the thing's capacity to mean anything at all.

A working rule falls out of combining the two: not every task deserves to be made easier. Some tasks earn every convenience available to them, without apology. Others deserve active protection from ease — kept effortful on purpose, because the effort was never the problem to be solved. The actual skill, the one this whole issue is circling, is learning to sort a life's worth of daily tasks into those two categories consciously, rather than letting a delivery app, a subscription, or plain habit sort them for you by default.

Bridge Frameworks are easy to nod along to in the abstract. The harder question is what this looks like in a life where almost everything genuinely can be made easier — and someone still has to decide, task by task, errand by errand, which ones should be.
05 Practice

5. In Practice

Choosing Your Friction, On Purpose

The problem with convenience today is not that too much of it exists. It is that almost none of it announces itself as optional. An app quietly turns a ten-minute walk to the hawker centre into a twenty-minute wait for a delivery eaten alone at a desk. A subscription turns the Saturday trip to the wet market — with its haggling, its familiar faces, its small daily negotiations — into a scheduled box that arrives while no one is looking. A helper turns a chore into someone else's task entirely. None of these substitutions is necessarily bad. Most of them are, on their own terms, small mercies.

And that is precisely what makes them difficult to evaluate. When everything is engineered toward less effort, effort itself stops functioning as a signal of importance. We stop asking whether a task deserved to be done by hand, and simply accept whichever version costs us least. Convenience, absorbed without examination, doesn't just save time — it quietly redraws the line between what counts as living and what counts as logistics.

Consider how easily this happens without any single bad decision along the way. A Sunday visit to grandparents gets shortened because a video call is “basically the same,” and infinitely more convenient for everyone's schedule. A child's argument with a friend, or a difficult piece of homework, gets resolved by a parent or a tuition app before the child has had the chance to sit with not-knowing long enough for it to teach them anything. A long-planned hike gets swapped for a mall with air-conditioning, because the mall is, undeniably, easier. Each choice is individually reasonable. The pattern they add up to is not.

None of this is an argument for making Sunday harder on principle, or for guilt-tripping a working parent for using every tool available to them. A delivery order on an exhausting Tuesday is simply sensible, and sensible is enough. The problem starts only when convenience stops being a tool reached for on purpose and becomes the automatic setting for everything — including the handful of things that actually needed the effort in order to mean anything at all.

The Common Places has no interest in prescribing which frictions to keep. That will look different for a new parent, a retiree, a shift worker, a founder — and it should. What we are interested in is a simple discipline, practised before the convenient option is chosen rather than after: ask what the effort was actually doing. Sometimes the honest answer is nothing — it was pure waste, and removing it is an unambiguous gain. Sometimes the honest answer is that the effort was the whole point, and the convenience, however tempting, was quietly going to cost you the thing you were trying to protect.

The goal was never to make life harder. It is to stop letting ease decide, by default and without your notice, what your life actually ends up being made of.

Takeaways & Further Reading

One Idea to Keep

Not every convenience is a shortcut worth taking. The people who use ease well are rarely the ones who reject it on principle — they are the ones who have decided, on purpose, exactly which parts of their life they refuse to make easier.

What We Chose Not to Include

We deliberately left out productivity techniques for “adding healthy friction” back into your life, digital detox routines, and minimalism guides. Those approaches can genuinely help. But technique was never the question this issue was built to answer. Before you optimise anything, the harder and prior question stands unanswered: which effort in your life was never actually a problem to be solved?

We also chose not to publish a straightforward list of “the best inconveniences to reintroduce.” That format performs the same trick convenience culture already plays — handing you a pre-packaged answer rather than a way of deciding for yourself. The purpose of this issue was never to tell you what to give up. It was to make the trade itself visible enough that you can start making it on purpose.

The Common Places is not trying to make your life harder, or easier. It is trying to help you notice, before the choice gets made for you, which parts of your life were never meant to be effortless.
