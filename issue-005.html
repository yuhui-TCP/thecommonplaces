ISSUE Nº005 — THE PERMISSION PROBLEM

If you already know it's time to stop, why do you keep waiting for someone else to say so?

The Permission Problem — why capable people wait for someone else to say it's okay to stop — and what that waiting quietly costs

For people who have quietly decided something needs to change and are still, months later, waiting for permission that was never going to arrive from outside. We begin with a question — about self-trust, authority, and who actually gets to decide when enough is enough — then follow the thread through its backstory, its complications, the ideas it opens up, and how they take shape in the real world.

Structure: 5-Step Journey Reading Time: ~18 min Subject: Self-Trust & Authority
01 Question

1. The Question

31 December, just past 6pm, the office mostly empty.

The leave balance sits at the bottom of the HR portal, the number in a shade of amber the system reserves for things you're about to lose. Fourteen days, unused, expiring at midnight — leave that was, by every written rule of the company, entirely yours to take, no justification required, no manager's blessing needed beyond the click of a button. And yet here you are, on the last working day of the year, having found a reason not to click that button every single month since March.

It was never that the work made it impossible. There were quiet weeks. There was a stretch in July where almost nothing urgent was happening at all. What was missing wasn't opportunity — it was something closer to a permit, an unmistakable, external sign that said now, it's fine, go. Nobody was going to send that sign. The leave policy was never written to include one. And so the days sat there, technically available, functionally frozen, until the system took them back on your behalf.

This is a strange kind of paralysis, because it isn't indecision in the ordinary sense. You weren't unsure whether you wanted the time off. You knew. What you were missing was something else entirely — the sense that wanting it was reason enough, that your own read of your own capacity could be trusted without a second signature.

Multiply that single leave balance across a career, and the shape of something larger starts to appear. The unsent resignation letter, drafted eight months before it's finally submitted. The promotion turned down twice before it's accepted once. The difficult conversation postponed until circumstances force it, rather than judgment choosing the moment. In each case, the person involved wasn't confused about what they wanted. They were waiting for a permission that no memo, no manager, and no institution was ever actually positioned to grant — because it was never theirs to give in the first place.

Call it the permission problem, to separate it from ordinary hesitation. Ordinary hesitation is genuine uncertainty, working itself out. The permission problem is certainty, fully formed, sitting idle — waiting for an authority that doesn't exist to confirm what's already been decided.

This is the question this issue exists to sit with: if you already know, why does knowing so rarely feel like enough to act on?

Bridge Before assuming this is simply a private failure of confidence, it's worth looking at what happens on the rare occasion someone takes this kind of permission for themselves, in public, where everyone can see it — and how quickly the crowd decides whether that was courage or something else.
02 Backstory

2. The Backstory

Simone Biles, Tokyo, 27 July 2021

Mid-competition, at the peak of her sport, the most decorated gymnast alive stepped back from the vault, walked off the competition floor, and did not return for the rest of the team final. There was no torn ligament, no visible injury for the cameras to find. When she explained herself afterward, in tears, what she described wasn't her body failing her — it was a decision, made in real time, that continuing wasn't safe for her mind. She described getting into a high-stress situation and freaking out, saying she had to focus on her mental health rather than risk her wellbeing.

The detail worth sitting with isn't the withdrawal itself — it's how little external permission existed for it. Biles later said her teammates initially wanted her to continue, reassuring her she'd be fine after watching her warm up, but she held her ground, telling them she couldn't risk the team's medal. There was no rulebook clause covering this, no coach with the authority to make the call for her. The permission, such as it was, had to come from her — in the middle of an Olympic final, with a global audience watching, and no one above her in the hierarchy who could have granted it on her behalf even if she'd asked.

The reaction split immediately, and predictably. Some called it the most powerful thing they'd seen an athlete do on that stage. Others called it quitting, weakness, letting the team down at the exact moment it mattered most. That split is the point. The withdrawal wasn't controversial because anyone seriously doubted Biles knew her own body and mind better than a stadium of spectators did. It was controversial because she gave herself something that almost nobody in that position is expected to give themselves — the authority to stop, on her own read of the situation, without waiting for it to be confirmed by someone else first.

Singapore has its own, much quieter version of the same dynamic, played out with far lower stakes and far less applause either way. It rarely takes the form of a dramatic public withdrawal. It looks more like the medical certificate people feel they need before taking a day off for exhaustion that isn't quite sickness — not because the leave policy requires a doctor's note for exhaustion, but because a note from someone else feels like the only legitimate form permission is allowed to take. The instinct is identical to what played out in Tokyo. It's simply been miniaturised, absorbed into ordinary life, and stripped of anyone paying enough attention to argue about it either way.

Bridge But it's worth taking the other side seriously before accepting self-permission as the obvious answer — because a system that runs on people pushing past the point of comfort doesn't survive if everyone starts calling their own stopping point at will.
03 Complication

3. The Complication

The Case Against Calling Your Own Number

In 2022, a term went viral that gave this instinct a name and, almost immediately, a backlash: quiet quitting — no longer going above and beyond at work, simply doing what the role required and nothing more. A Randstad survey around the same period found roughly 35 percent of Singaporean workers reporting they had quietly quit, slightly above the global average — a genuinely large number of people, independently, arriving at the same small act of self-permission: deciding, without asking, that they'd done enough.

The response to that number was not, on the whole, admiration. The trend was quickly framed as a Gen Z problem — lazy, entitled, less committed than the generations before them — pitting younger workers against older ones in a debate that treated the behaviour as a character flaw rather than a boundary. The pattern is the same one Biles ran into in Tokyo, just slower and more diffuse: the moment self-permission becomes visible at scale, the instinctive public response is to treat it as the earliest warning sign of decline, not as evidence that something upstream needed adjusting.

And the uncomfortable part is that the sceptics aren't entirely wrong to worry. A workplace, a family, a country doesn't function if everyone simply stops the instant something gets difficult. Singapore's own account of itself leans hard on the opposite instinct — the generation that kept going past the point of comfort, because stopping was never treated as a serious option. If self-permission is lionised without qualification, what's left to stop it from becoming a permission slip for anything at all?

What's telling is what actually happened next, once quiet quitting had been named and publicly judged. Rather than more people simply lying flat, the more common response in Singapore has been quieter and more contradictory still: “job hugging,” clinging to roles people no longer believe in out of fear of instability, alongside “revenge bedtime procrastination” — stealing back late-night hours from a life otherwise governed by optimisation. In other words: even once permission had a name, most people didn't take it. They kept the job they wanted to leave and stole the rest they wouldn't ask for outright — permission taken in secret, after hours, rather than claimed in the open.

That pattern is the real complication. It isn't that self-permission is dangerous in principle. It's that almost nobody actually exercises it cleanly, even once it's been named, discussed, and surveyed at scale. Most people instead find a smaller, quieter workaround — permission taken sideways, unannounced — which suggests the problem was never really a shortage of information about the option. It was something closer to what Biles ran into on the floor in Tokyo: an absence of felt authority to use it.

Bridge If the permission genuinely has to come from inside, and no policy or public discussion can hand it over ready-made, the harder question is what actually makes that internal authority feel real, rather than borrowed or invented on the spot.
04 Idea

4. The Idea

The Permission Slip — Brené Brown

In her 2017 book Braving the Wilderness, researcher Brené Brown describes getting ready to meet Oprah Winfrey for the first time — a genuinely high-stakes professional moment — and finding herself paralysed by the pressure to be composed, serious, impressive. Her daughter had texted, reminding her to sign a permission slip for a school trip, and sitting on the edge of her bed, Brown realised she needed exactly that for herself: something that would formally grant her permission to stop being so serious and afraid, to have fun that day instead. She wrote it on a Post-it note: permission to be excited and goofy and to have fun — then walked into the meeting having, in effect, signed her own note.

What makes this more than a charming anecdote is what it reveals about how permission actually works. Brown's practice — now used widely in her workshops with teams and leaders — treats permission not as something to be granted top-down, but as an act you can perform on yourself, deliberately, with the same weight a signed authorisation carries when it comes from someone else. The insight isn't that you don't need permission. It's that permission was never structurally required to come from outside you in the first place — it only ever felt that way because almost nobody had shown you it could be issued from the inside.

This reframes both the Biles moment and the quiet-quitting backlash in the same light. Biles didn't skip the step of getting permission — she simply granted it to herself, out loud, under maximum scrutiny, instead of waiting for a coach or a federation to do it for her. The quiet quitters who quietly went back to job hugging weren't lacking the concept; they were lacking the felt sense that self-issued permission counts as much as the kind that comes with someone else's signature on it. The self-authorised version feels flimsy, almost illegitimate, right up until the moment someone actually uses it and discovers the world doesn't collapse.

A useful distinction falls out of this: there's a real difference between permission you haven't given yourself and discipline you've genuinely chosen. Someone who could stop, has clearly and honestly granted themselves permission to stop, and keeps going anyway — that's discipline, freely exercised. Someone who keeps going only because stopping has never felt like a legitimate option is not being disciplined. They're simply waiting for an authority that was never going to show up, because the position was vacant, and always had been.

Bridge Frameworks are easy to nod along to in the abstract. The harder question is what this looks like the next time you're the one staring at an unused leave balance, an unsent letter, or a “no” you've been meaning to say — with no one standing by to sign off on it for you.
05 Practice

5. In Practice

Issuing Your Own Slip

The problem with self-permission is that it never arrives labelled as sufficient. A resignation you've been meaning to hand in can feel, right up until the moment you do it, like something that still needs one more validating conversation, one more sign, one more person telling you it's the right call. That feeling is not information about whether the decision is correct. It's simply what an unfamiliar kind of authority feels like the first few times you exercise it yourself.

Consider how naturally the alternative sets in without a single bad decision along the way. The leave balance disappears one policy cycle at a time, deferred because there was always something that seemed to need you more. The difficult conversation gets postponed because the moment never feels quite ready, and readiness, on inspection, was always going to be a feeling you'd have to supply yourself. The pattern these small deferrals add up to — a life in which nothing meaningful gets decided until circumstances decide it for you — is not a character flaw. It's simply what happens by default when permission is treated as something that has to be found outside, in a place where it was never actually kept.

None of this is an argument for acting on every impulse the moment it arrives, or for pretending that some decisions genuinely do benefit from more information, more time, or another person's perspective. Wanting a second opinion isn't the same failure as this issue is describing — that's actual deliberation, and it has its place. The problem starts only when the thing you're waiting for isn't more information at all, but simply someone else's signature on a decision you've already, privately, made.

The Common Places has no interest in telling you which decisions deserve your permission. What we're interested in is a simple, quieter test, applied honestly the next time you notice yourself waiting: if permission had already been granted — by whoever you're unconsciously waiting on — would you already know what to do? If the honest answer is yes, the waiting isn't deliberation. It's a permission slip you've already written in your head and haven't let yourself sign.

The goal was never to act without thought, or to treat every hesitation as a failure of nerve. It is to notice when the authority you're waiting for was never going to arrive from outside — because the position was yours to fill the entire time, and no one else was ever standing behind that desk.

What We Chose Not to Include

We deliberately left out productivity frameworks for setting boundaries, negotiation scripts for asking your manager for time off, and “how to say no” guides. Those tools can genuinely help once permission has already been granted. But technique was never the question this issue was built to answer — the harder and prior question is why the permission felt unavailable in the first place, long before any script was needed to act on it.

We also chose not to publish a checklist of “signs you need to give yourself permission to rest.” That format hands over a diagnosis instead of a practice — the same trick the permission problem already plays on you, dressed up as self-help. The purpose of this issue was never to tell you when to stop. It was to make visible that the authority to decide was never going to come from anywhere but you.

The Common Places is not trying to tell you what to do with your own permission. It is trying to help you notice, the next time you're waiting for a sign, that you may already be standing in front of the only desk that was ever going to issue one.
