ISSUE Nº006 — THE APOLOGY ECONOMY

If saying “I've been so busy” is meant as an apology, why does it so often sound like a boast — and why do we keep accepting both?

Why “sorry, I've been so busy” is an apology and a boast at once — and what we do by letting it pass

For people who have said “sorry, I've been so busy” and meant two things by it — and for those who have nodded along without quite hearing either. We begin with a question — about status, apology, and who actually keeps the books — then follow the thread through its backstory, its complications, the ideas it opens up, and how they take shape in the real world.

Structure: 5-Step Journey Reading Time: ~18 min Subject: Status & Apology
01 Question

1. The Question

Sunday, just before 10pm, a message eleven days old.

The reply lands at the bottom of an old thread, directly beneath your own question about dinner. Sorry for the slow reply — it's been so busy at work lately. Let's catch up soon! You read it once, and your thumbs are already moving. No worries at all, I totally get it. Same here! Sent. The thread goes quiet, and the dinner, you both understand, will not be happening this month.

Nothing about this exchange was unkind. It happens many times a week, in group chats and corridors and the first line of countless emails, and it feels like the very definition of good manners. But look at what the message actually did. It owed you something — a reply, a date, some sign you had been on the sender's mind — and it paid that debt with an apology. In the same sentence, it also told you something else: that the sender's days are full, their attention is in demand, their time is too scarce to spend on you. The first half of the message was an apology. The second half was an announcement. And your answer, I totally get it, accepted both.

This is a strange kind of transaction, because nobody in it is lying. The sender may well have been busy. You may well understand. What is odd is that the sentence works as an apology precisely because it also works as a boast — an admission that would sound faintly shameful in plain words (I put you below everything else this month) arrives instead dressed as a credential.

Multiply that single message across a working life, and the shape of something larger starts to appear. The “how are you?” in the corridor, answered with “slammed”. The lunch declined because things are “crazy right now”. The email that opens with “apologies for the delay, back-to-back all week”. In each case, someone is settling what they owe with a currency they are also, quietly, earning from.

Call it the apology economy, to separate it from ordinary apology. An ordinary apology costs the speaker something: it admits a lapse, and the admission stings, which is partly how you know it is sincere. In the apology economy, the cost is refunded in the same breath. The admission arrives with interest.

This is the question this issue exists to sit with: if “I've been so busy” settles a debt and raises the speaker's standing at the same time, why do we — the people being apologised to — so reliably let it through?

02 Backstory

2. The Backstory

Jack Ma and “996”, 2019

In 2019, the founder of Alibaba — one of the most admired entrepreneurs in China — told the world what he thought of working from nine in the morning to nine at night, six days a week. The pattern, shorthand “996”, adds up to seventy-two hours. He did not describe it as a regrettable necessity of building a company. He called it a blessing, an opportunity that many firms and their employees never get, and asked, in effect, when else a young person was supposed to do it. If you love the work, he suggested, the hours are not a problem. Richard Liu, the founder of JD.com, went further: people who idle their days away, he said, were no brothers of his.

The detail worth sitting with isn't the hours — long hours were hardly news in a competitive industry. It's the grammar. The schedule was not offered as a cost, or even as a bargain. It was offered as a privilege, something to be grateful for and, by implication, something to be envied. Busyness was no longer the thing you apologised for. It was the thing you were lucky to have, and it made the headlines on its own.

The reaction split almost at once. Tech workers organised online protests against 996; others heard exactly the ambition they admired. In August 2021, China's top court and its labour ministry jointly declared the practice illegal, after reviewing ten cases — among them a courier company employee who had been dismissed for refusing the schedule, and whom the court sided with. Promoting hard work is perfectly fine, the court said; it simply cannot become a shield for employers who want to dodge their legal obligations. A message that began as encouragement ended up in a courtroom, which is a long way for a compliment to travel.

None of this is really a story about China. Ma said loudly what most of us say softly. A founder who calls seventy-two-hour weeks a blessing and a friend who texts “so busy, sorry!” are standing on the same premise: that being busy marks you as worth something, and that the unbusy have somehow missed out. One says it from a stage. The other tucks it inside an apology. The grammar is the same; only the scale has shrunk.

Singapore's version is quieter, and it mostly arrives with a smile. It rarely sounds like a speech. It sounds like the lunch that can't happen because things are hectic at the moment, the reply that begins with an apology for the delay, the reflexive “busy, busy” offered when a colleague asks how you've been. The hours behind it are real: ADP's People at Work survey for 2024 found Singapore employees clocking an average of eight additional, unpaid hours a week. What's worth noticing is what the sentence does with those hours. It turns them into something you are sorry about and, softly, something you are proud of.

Bridge Which invites a tidy conclusion: we are all, in our small ways, showing off. It's the obvious reading — and it's worth testing before accepting it.
03 Complication

3. The Complication

The Case for the Full Calendar

In 2012, the writer Tim Kreider put the tidy conclusion into print. In an essay for the New York Times called “The Busy Trap”, he described the standard “I'm so busy” as a “boast disguised as a complaint”. The people who say it, he noticed, are rarely the ones working back-to-back hospital shifts or commuting between three low-paid jobs; those people aren't busy, they're tired. The people who talk about being busy are almost always the ones whose busyness is self-imposed, and the stock reply — that's a good problem to have — is a kind of congratulation. The essay was widely shared. Few disputed it.

And yet the diagnosis, accurate as it is, doesn't quite explain why the habit is so hard to drop. Picture a workplace where people compete for the same promotions, the same clients, the same few minutes of the same decision-makers' attention. In that setting, a packed calendar says something useful. It tells everyone watching that other people want your time — and therefore that you must be valuable. An empty calendar says the opposite without anyone having to accuse you of anything. The silence raises the question on its own.

Economists call this signaling. Michael Spence, who later shared a Nobel Prize for the idea, showed that a university degree can be valuable partly because it tells an employer something about you that is hard to see directly. What makes a signal believable is that it is hard to fake, and a full diary is very hard to fake for long. You either have people asking for your time or you don't.

In Singapore, the cost of looking less busy shows up in the numbers. The same ADP survey found that almost half of Singapore employees feel judged for using flexible work arrangements — arrangements that employers are now expected to consider fairly. Where visible presence is read as commitment, the person who is visibly less busy pays for it, whatever they actually deliver.

What happens next is that the signal escalates. Once everyone around you is busy, being merely busy stops carrying any information; you have to be busier, or at least sound it. Try telling a colleague, cheerfully, that you've had loads of free time lately. The small wince you feel before you say it is the system working. In a competitive room, free time is something you hide.

That is the real complication. The person typing “so busy, sorry!” is neither a fraud nor a victim. They are responding sensibly to an incentive that is real, which is why the usual advice — stop glorifying busyness, protect your time — has such a poor record. It asks the speaker to walk out of a market that is still paying everyone else. Whatever is holding the sentence in place, it cannot be only the speaker.

Bridge If the speaker can't easily step out of the exchange, the question becomes who else is party to it — and what would change if that person stopped playing along.
04 Idea

4. The Idea

The Double Entry

In bookkeeping, a double entry records every transaction in two places at once: one account falls, another rises, and both entries come from a single event. “Sorry, I've been so busy” works the same way. The first entry is the apology, which acknowledges a debt — you waited, and you were not the priority. The second is a credit to the speaker's standing: busy means in demand, and in demand means valuable. The second entry quietly refunds the cost of the first, which is why the sentence is so easy to say.

The best evidence that this is more than a metaphor comes from a 2017 study in the Journal of Consumer Research. Silvia Bellezza of Columbia Business School and her co-authors, Neeru Paharia and Anat Keinan, set out to test what they called the conspicuous consumption of time. A century earlier, the economist Thorstein Veblen had described a leisure class whose status came from visibly not working. Bellezza and her colleagues found that, in the United States at least, the logic had reversed: people who signaled busyness and a lack of leisure were seen as higher in status. In Italy, the effect flipped back, and a leisurely life still read as standing. The authors explained the shift as a move from the scarcity of goods to the scarcity of individuals. In a world where people are the precious resource, being hard to reach becomes a luxury. Even products could carry the signal: a grocery delivery service, associated with saving time, signaled status about as well as an expensive supermarket.

An honest limit is worth stating. Those studies measured how people judge one another, using mostly American and Italian samples. Nobody has measured it here, and it would be a mistake to pretend otherwise. What the research does establish is more modest and more useful: that the meaning of “busy” is not fixed. If it buys status in one place and loses it in another, it is an agreement, not a law of nature.

This reframes both the Ma episode and the Singapore numbers in the same light. Ma wasn't describing a fact about work; he was quoting an exchange rate, and the court ruling is what it looks like when that rate is disputed. The respondents who feel judged for stepping out of the long-hours convention are describing the same rate being enforced from the other side.

But the half of the exchange that gets the least attention is the second one. A boast needs an audience that accepts it. Kreider spotted the tell: the stock reply to “busy” is a congratulation. The speaker writes the entry; the listener signs it off. This is the shift from what we discussed in Issue 005. There, the authority you were waiting for turned out to be yours to issue. Here, the currency you have been accepting turns out to be yours to decline.

A useful distinction falls out of this: there is a real difference between busyness as information and busyness as currency. Someone who tells you honestly that they are at the limit of what they can carry is giving you information, and it deserves a real hearing. Someone who uses the same words to cancel a debt and raise their standing is spending currency. The sentence is identical. The only way to tell the difference is by what happens when you don't automatically absolve it.

Bridge Ideas like this are easy to nod along to in the abstract. The harder question is what changes the next time that message arrives on your phone, and your thumbs are already moving.
05 Practice

5. In Practice

Reading the Receipt

The problem with absolving by reflex is that it never feels like a decision. “No worries, I totally get it” is built to be typed before the message has finished being read. It feels like kindness, and often it is. That is exactly why nothing ever interrupts it.

Consider how naturally the alternative sets in without a single unkind act along the way. The dinner is never rescheduled, because each let's catch up soon is met with another yes, soon. The currency keeps circulating, and nothing gets bought. A year on, you find that you and a friend have been on each other's radar as a courtesy, without quite being in each other's lives — a friendship maintained entirely in apologies, and nowhere else.

None of this is an argument for interrogating friends, or for treating every “busy” as a boast. People are sometimes genuinely stretched, and accepting an apology graciously is a real kindness; the habit worth examining is the reflex, not the grace. Nor is it an argument that you stand above the game. You are, in all likelihood, sending the same message to someone else this week.

The Common Places has no interest in telling you how to answer. What we're interested in is a simple, quieter test, applied the next time the sentence arrives and before you type a word: if “busy” weren't an available word, what would this message have to say instead? Perhaps I put you below other things this month — which is information about where you stand, and worth knowing. Perhaps I forgot, or I didn't know what to say. Perhaps, simply, I'm at my limit — which deserves a real response rather than a pass. Whatever it is, it is information, and information can be answered. What the reflex does is answer the performance instead.

If you want to run the test as an experiment, try it for a week. Notice each time the sentence reaches you, and hold back the automatic reassurance. Answer what the message is actually reporting — a real question about when, or whether — or let a pause do the work. By the end of the week you will likely have a clearer sense of which people in your life are sending you information, and which are paying a toll.

The goal was never to refuse anyone's apology. It is to notice that the exchange you have been joining as a bystander was always a two-party transaction, and that the ledger only balanced because you kept signing it. The pen was in your hand the entire time.

Issue Nº006 Takeaways

One Idea to Keep

“Busy” was never only an apology. It was a payment — and a payment only clears when the person on the other end accepts it.

What We Chose Not to Include

We deliberately left out time-management systems, scripts for declining invitations gracefully, and “how to say you're busy without sounding busy” guides. Those tools can genuinely help once the exchange itself is understood. But technique was never the question this issue was built to answer — the prior question is why a sentence that costs the speaker nothing is accepted as payment, long before anyone needs a better way to say it.

We also chose not to publish a checklist of phrases that give away performative busyness. That format hands over a diagnosis instead of a practice, and it would turn this issue into one more way of judging other people's calendars — which is, more or less, the habit it set out to examine. The purpose was never to catch anyone out. It was to make visible that the ledger has two sides, and that you were always holding one of them.

The Common Places is not trying to tell you whether to be busy, or whether to forgive those who are. It is trying to help you notice, the next time someone says it, that your answer was always part of what the sentence meant.
